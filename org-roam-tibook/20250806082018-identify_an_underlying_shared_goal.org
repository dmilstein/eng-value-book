** Ask Why But Be Ready For It To Not Work

The simplest method for identifying a shared underlying goal is simply to ask "Why?"

And that's well worth trying!

If you're a tech lead working long hours for an upcoming launch, ask your product manager, "Why are we trying to ship this new feature by the end of next month?"

If you're leading an engineering team that owns new account set up, ask your CEO, "Why, exactly, is reducing onboarding time our most important goal for this year?"

If you're a senior engineer who joins a company and promptly discovers that everyone is toiling away in the midst of an endless rewrite, ask your CTO, "Why, exactly, are we doing the big replatforming?"

If you're lucky, your stakeholder will take the time to explain the underlying reason you're being asked to do whatever it is you do at your job. Echo back and summarize what you hear, and, if you still have questions, ask "Why?" again. Before long, you should be able to find an underlying goal that your stakeholder cares a great deal about, and which you can happily sign up to achieve. And, by echoing back and summarizing, you'll have found a way to state it that your stakeholder finds compelling.

But, unfortunately, simply asking "Why?" doesn't always work.

Why not?

# Let me tell you a secret:

Because most stakeholders spend their days struggling with anxiety about achieving their near-term goals.

If you directly ask such a stakeholder, "Why are we doing this project?" that anxiety can flare up. They might try to quickly shut down the conversation to regain control. They might patronizingly announce, "Don't worry about that. You need to just finish the tasks in front of you."

I'd love to tell you that most Important People feel pleasantly secure in their positions of authority, and therefore welcome probing questions about why, but, um, I'm your friend and I don't want to lie to you?

** Two Tactics For Drawing Out Underlying Goals

Here are a pair of tactics to help you bring your stakeholder's long-term goals out into the open, even if they seem resistant to discussing anything other than why you're not done with your JIRA tickets yet. We'll first sketch both in, then bring them to life with a story.

The first helps address the challenge of getting your person talking in the first place, the second encourages them to open up about their hopes and dreams, aka, their goals.

# Let's talk about a trio of tactics that can help you bring your stakeholder's long-term goals out into the open, even if they are initially resistant to discussing anything other than when you'll be done with your JIRA tickets.

*** Tactic 1: Repeat Trailing Words

/When someone stops talking, but you want to know more, repeat the last three words they said, calmly and curiously. Then, stop talking and wait./

This is an absurdly simple hack! It works kind of shockingly well!

If you can stop yourself from saying anything other than those three words, and you can wait just a bit longer than feels comfortable...

...the person will start talking again, telling you more and more.

And, believe it or not, they will /feel/ like you're hearing and understanding them.

# It shouldn't be this easy. But it totally, totally is.

*** Tactic 2: Invoke A Positive Vision Of The Future

/Invite your stakeholder to imagine a happy moment in the future when the current work has all been successfully completed. Create a deliberately playful image, like, "We're toasting champagne." Then ask, "What good things have been unlocked? What is now better for our business? Why are we celebrating?"/

If, instead of asking for goals, you invite them to /imagine/ a happy longer-term future, you create a space where they can safely put aside their near-term fears. By throwing in playful, oddball details, you trick their mind into loosening up.

# Invoking an oddball, specific image like "throwing a big party" engages the playful side of their mind.[fn:: Product Managers use a very similar tactic in customer interviews when they say "If you could wave a magic wand and poof!, things were suddenly much better, what would have improved?"] When you then echo back and summarize, you help them feel like you are /with/ them in facing their fears, while also obtaining enough information to be ready to make a useful proposal for change.

** How To Practice Repeat Trailing Words

This one is simple enough that I recommend initially practicing it multiple times a day.

Whenever you can, in whatever conversations you find yourself in, try repeating the last few words someone says to you, inflected as a question. Try it in both professional and personal contexts, try it with your manager, try it with your peers, with your spouse, kids, neighbors.

As you do so, try to:

 - Allow yourself to be in a place of genuine curiosity

 - Say /only/ the last three or four words back

 - Wait silently for a response, even if it feels uncomfortable

Practice over and over. Take some time to reflect on how it feels, and use that reflection to tweak your personal approach.

As you build comfort, you'll find that you can go through multiple rounds of this, in a single conversation, and often get surprisingly deep. If you find you've learned something interesting or moving, try Echoing Back & Summarizing and/or Offering Labeled Feelings.

For an initial, focused period of practice to rewire yourself, aim to do this at least two to three times a day, every day for a week.

** How To Practice Painting A Positive Vision Of The Future

A key here is to find a personal, authentic way to inject liberating playfulness into your questions.

Our example above was:

/"Let's imagine: it's 18 to 24 months from now. The business has done incredibly well. We're all happily celebrating and toasting champagne. What good thing has happened? What is the success that we're toasting?"/

Try that one out. See if you can find a way to share a sense of lightness as you state it. If not, play around with it until it feels easy and a bit fun to offer. Ideally, you want to let yourself be just a bit silly, to signal to your listener that it's okay to let go of fears for a moment.

Try it out with friends and peers (at your own company, or elsewhere!). Echo back and summarize what you hear.

There are many, many mediocre goals, outcomes, key results, and intents out there.

Luckily, there's a razor sharp test of a good strategic outcome:

 - A good strategic outcome is one that allows people across the company to quickly make good decisions

(I encountered the idea of strategy enabling decisions in Stephen Bungay's The Art of Action, and it has, honestly, kind of changed my life)

Unfortunately, in my experience, the vast majority of companies building software do not set strategy in a way that allows for rapid on-the-ground decision-making. Rather, it's a set of aspirational goals, without any opinionated guidance on how to get to those goals. Or a set of clearly conflicting priorities, with no way to weight them against each other.

If you are trying to come up with a clean, effective statement of strategy, here are two excellent questions to ask yourself:

# I'll offer a simple lens for you to use, as you go about trying to develop and refine your understanding of a strategic outcome:

 - Is it possible to *disagree* with your statement of strategy?

 - Does the strategy allow you to say *"No"* to things?

These two questions ensure you avoid impressive-sounding by meaningless [toothless] strategies like "Accelerate growth" or "Become more customer centric". Who could possibly disagree with those? What would they possibly allow you to say "no" to?

You want to relentlessly refine such vague statements until they become sharper, clearer and more opinionated. For example, you might turn "Accelerate growth" into "Accelerate growth by enabling more upsells to existing customers".

# XXX Experiment with weaving back in Maria's story

That's a statement that someone could absolutely disagree with. Such a person might make a case for expanding your Total Addressable Market (TAM), instead of capturing more revenue from existing customers. That's a valid alternative strategy. If you can't imagine a valid alternative strategy, you're running some real risk of developing a statement too vague and aspirational to be useful.

If your company committed to "Accelerate growth by enabling more upsells to existing customers" that would absolutely let a team on the ground say "No" to things. When an excited sales leader shows up at the weekly demo and demands the team add a feature to allow them to sell into a new segment, the product manager can calmly refer to that intent to push back.

Fundamentally, a good strategic outcome allows you to *make decisions*.

** Sidebar: A Theory Of Why Most Goals Are Terrible

Here's the thing: developing software is weird as hell.

And, at the heart of that weirdness is: to successfully develop software, the team on the ground has to make a metric ton of *decisions*.

Like, way, way more than a team working on a typical non-software business project.

I don't say this to slag people working in sales, marketing or other critical business functions. Those jobs are extremely hard, and require a great deal of skill and experience to do well. Some of the smartest, most effective leaders I've ever known came up through marketing and sales.

But the special weirdness of software around both the quantity and the scope of decision-making is not shared by most business functions.

A team that spends six or twelve months building a valuable piece of software will have, over that time, made hundreds of decisions to:

 - Hone in on exactly the business problem that software should solve

 - Iteratively explore features that could solve that problem

 - Identify which of those features can be built in a reasonable period of time

 - Find a safe, maintainable way to build those features

Crucially, all those decisions effect each other. If it proves harder than expected to build something, the team has to revisit their decision about what features to build. If early product testing reveals a key constraint that customers forgot to share, the team has to revisit a whole slew of decisions.

You can't make all the big decisions up front, and leave the executional details to the team.

Successful teams make decisions of different scopes, all the time.

They must be able to make a lot of those decisions *without asking their boss*.

Unfortunately, most goals fail on this front, for one of two reasons:

 - Insufficient context

 - Unclear prioritization

The former means: some stakeholder has decided what the team "should do", and they just tell the team to do that thing. They don't pass on why that thing is important, alternatives considered, risks to avoid, upstream dependencies, or downstream consequences, etc, etc. This cripples the team's ability to rapidly make good decisions.

The latter means: rather than face hard tradeoffs between competing priorities, a stakeholder simply assigns separate goals to separate teams, and washes their hands of the ensuing chaos and slowdowns.

# Also assign this to Maria's company

Company facing a hard choice between keeping existing customers happy and developing a new product? No problem, make the former the goal of the Account Management team, and the latter the goal of the Product Team, no tradeoffs needed. Until, of course, the product team's work grinds to a halt, because the AM team is constantly pressuring them to support existing customers, because they've been told that's the most important thing for the business. The product team can't make a quick decision to ignore the AM's -- they have to loop in the Nearest Common Ancestor of their team and the AM's. Which makes all those decisions take forever.

The lens of decision speed is incredibly useful.

The reason this is such a common form of conflict with The Rest Of The Business is that the uncertainty around developing software is just so much greater than around most business activities. It's a form of research as much as executional operation.

Please go read Principles of Product Development Flow, by Don Reinertsen if you would like to be blinded by the science of this.

** Tactic One: Invoke A Positive Vision Of The Future.

For this, you invite your stakeholder to imagine that all the current tasks, projects, and milestones have been finished brilliantly and well. You then ask them what good thing will have become true. By invoking the silly-sounding specific image of "toasting champagne", you engage the playful side of their mind.[fn:: PM's use a very similar tactic in customer interviews when they say "If you could wave a magic wand and poof!, things were suddenly much better, what would have improved?"]

Having created a sense of safety by imagining the happy outcome, you can now tack back to things that might worry them. This allows you to tease out how your stakeholder is working backwards from the strategic outcome to the day-to-day work of your team. # The Anatomy Of An Effective Statement Of Strategy

#  There were three parts to what Maria e

Maria skillfully employed a wealth of tactics for building rapport (many of which we'll return to, in [[id:5C7A0B37-8984-4A1F-8371-C1FCEB637174][How To Make a Compelling Offer]]).

We'll call such an understanding "A Statement Of Strategy". We'll specifically push for a statement of strategy that allows you and your stakeholder to make hard decisions. To do so, it should have three key parts:

 - First, it must be rooted in a *key business outcome*

   e.g. Grow Enterprise ARR to 30% of total revenue, within 18 months

 - Next, it identifies a *core challenge* to achieving that outcome

   Build an enterprise sales pipeline and move customers through it

 - Finally, it specifies your team's *role* in overcoming that core challenge

   Add new, enterprise-specific features to unblock sales conversations

That three-part breakdown is extremely powerful. It concisely maps from the highest level goals of the business to the work your team is doing -- while retaining the flexibility to adapt and change details as you go.

Most centrally, an effective statement of strategy must allow people throughout a company to quickly *make decisions*, without having to go through slow-moving reviews up the management chain.

To achieve this, the statement must be extremely *opionionated*. It can't list some sprawling set of vaguely conflicting goals. It should be so opinionated that it feels genuinely uncomfortable, and even a bit scary, to commit to it.

Thus, you should always ask the following two questions of a potential statement of strategy:

 - Is it possible to *disagree* with the statement?

 - Would committing to it allow you to say *"No"* to things?

These two questions ensure you avoid impressive-sounding by toothless strategies like "Be more customer centric". Vague, aspirational strategies don't allow people to make decisions---they just create endless arguments. There isn't an executive alive who won't make a case that their pet project is necessary for your company to "be more customer centric".

** "What You Don't Know Won't Distract You"

Many stakeholders deliberately limit what they share in an attempt to encourage you to "focus on what matters". They don't want you to be "distracted" by what they consider to be irrelevant information.

It's important to realize that this approach is not necessarily dumb or crazy. This exact approach may have served your stakeholder quite well when running *non-software* projects. Such projects often *can* be broken down into largely independent parts, that play out more or less as planned. It's often better in such cases for a leader to spend most of their time with each team on the details of those parts, not on the far-distant big picture.

The bad news is that only talking about tasks just completely does not work for software projects.

The good news is that, if you can learn a little bit about the longer-term goal, you can almost always offer your stakeholder some creative options to get there just a bit faster. Which will feel good to them. Which will then make them willing to share *more* details around their long-term goals. Which will let you come up even with more creative options. And around you can go.

If you do an even half-decent job of summarizing back what you hear, they will be visibly relieved that someone seems to understand what they're trying to do.

And you'll be ready to move on to later steps in the overall game plan.

You can only make such constant adaptations if you truly understand the why of the work you're doing. And the three-part formulation of an actionable statement of strategy is one of the best ways I know how to understand that why.

With a friend, spend some time talking out the strategy of your business. Build as clear a picture as you can of:

 - The most important thing your business is trying to achieve, over the next few years

 - The core challenge the business must face, on the path to that goal

 - How your team will play a role in overcoming that challenge

Try to develop a few different formulations of the above. Work to make them as sharp and clear as you possibly can.

If your company is large, you might map out a strategy for your sub-part of the company, instead of the company as a whole. But push yourself to expand your perspective, to look behind the objectives of the group or division or team you work in day-to-day.

As you do this work, continually ask:

 - Is it possible to *disagree* with the statement I'm making?

 - Would committing to the statement allow me to say *"No"* to things?

# Often, the statement will feel usefully uncomfortable.

As an experiment, try making your statement of intent "frighteningly opinionated": so sharp and clear that, if you committed to it, your team would have to immediately stop doing some things that "everyone knows" are critically important. If you're able to achieve this kind of clarirty, slow down and really probe at the activities you've now called into question. Are they truly important? If so, why? This will either turn up a refinement of your statement of strategy (because those activities are genuinely critical), or identify a valuable change you could advocate for (because those activities are a distraction).

** How To Practice Developing An Actionable Statement of Strategy
*** Offer A Positive Vision Of The Future

A key here is to find a personal, authentic way to inject liberating playfulness into your questions.

Our example above was:

/"Let's imagine: it's 18 to 24 months from now. The business has done incredibly well. We're all happily celebrating and toasting champagne. What good thing has happened? What is the success that we're toasting?"/

Try that one out. See if you can find a way to share a sense of lightness as you state it. If not, play around with it until it feels easy and a bit fun to offer. Ideally, you want to let yourself be just a bit silly, to signal to your listener that it's okay to let go of fears for a moment.

Try it out with friends and peers (at your own company, or elsewhere!). Echo back and summarize what you hear.

Make sure you can smoothly offer the follow ups, adjusting to make them work for you:

/"What is the key challenge to overcome to arrive at that good thing? Or, to put it another way, if it's 18-24 months out, and things have gone wrong, what, specifically might have happened?"/

/"What role will my team play in overcoming that challenge? How will we know if we're succeeding?"/

*** Make A Usefully Wrong Guess

Do this after going through one of the exercises above. You've either come up with your own statement of strategy, or used the positive vision of the future to draw your stakeholder out.

Practice sharing the statement of strategy itself, as clearly and concisely as you can. Push yourself to make it usefully too opinionated.

Be sure to rehearse the question you'll ask at the end, where you invite them to tell you you're wrong. Our example was:

/"Do I have that even vaguely right? Am I missing something important?"/

Try that out. Adjust the wording to make it feel natural.

Once you feel comfortable going through the full arc, share your usefully wrong guess with your stakeholder, and end with your invitation to critique. Make sure you're able to ask the questions at the end authentically. Allow yourself to channel a true curiosity.

Once they share something, echo back what you hear, adjust and try again.

For further practice, talk this out with friends who work at other companies. Ask what their business is trying to achieve, ask about fundamental challenges they must face, brainstorm different ways their teams might help overcome those challenges. Look for something usefully opinionated. Offer that to them, invite them to tell you if you're wrong, adjust and try again.
** Don't Be Surprised If This Is Harder Than You Expect

# Developing A Statement Of Strategy Is Not Easy

Finally, I'll note: developing a coherent, concise and compelling statement of strategy is no kind of easy.

# *hard*.

The reality facing any business is always extremely complex. Trying to distill that down to a single core challenge takes real work. There are dozens of factors you could consider, dozens of problems people are worrying about, different hopes and dreams different people across the company are treasuring in their hearts.

As you work to develop a single, clear statement of strategy for your business, you may find that it's surprisingly challenging. That's okay!

Building such a clear picture is a *learnable skill*, but it does takes real practice.

** Tactic Three: Offer Two Ends Of A Spectrum
This is a variant on the Usefully Wrong Guess that I find particularly useful if you're hitting a total brick wall with someone. Here's the approach:

 - Guess two different, potentially valid answers to the question

 - Offer them as "ends of a spectrum"

 - Ask the stakeholder where they think things land on that spectrum

Part of why this works so well is that you are, once again, offering the stakeholder an opportunity to tell you you're wrong... but to do so in a way which allows you to fill in your picture of what their long-term goals are.

Let's say that Janine is an engineer at a company that sells ecommerce enablement tools. One afternoon, she finds herself in a conversation with the CEO, and takes the opportunity to try fill in her picture of overall company goals (this is a /super/ valuable habit to build, by the way).

"I'm pretty excited," she says, "about how Cogs 2.0 is coming together, on the engineering side. But, I wanted to ask: when we finally *do* migrate all the stores, what's the win you're most excited to see?"

The CEO tilts his head. "What do you mean?"

"Well", says Janine, "we've been working on it for so long, I've heard lots of different things people are hoping for. I'm just curious: what's the thing you, specifically, are most excited about?"

The CEO gives a small laugh. "Having the project be over?" He shrugs. "There's a lot, I don't if I can narrow it down." Having dispensed this executive wisdom, he seems to be done.

 Janine could certainly give up at this point. But she could also take a shot at using our new tactic.

"I could almost imagine," she says calmly, "two different ends of a spectrum." She holds up her left hand. "On the one hand, I've heard that, once we've got all the stores on 2.0, it's going to simplify new store set up a *ton*.  The Success team won't have to build an inventory plan from scratch every time, we can use templates, it'll be both faster and cheaper. So that should save us a bunch of money, and maybe let us go after some smaller stores."

"But," and now she hold up her right hand, showing a space between it and her left, "I've also heard a lot of excitement about, once 2.0 is out, being able to create new enterprise features for high-volume stores. Which will let us add a pro tier, charge more, increase revenue per store."

"Is it more one of those than the other? Or some mix of the two? Or something else?"

The CEO is going to hear this, and it's going to be hard for them to not jump in and respond. They might pick one end, they might pick a mix, or they might reject the spectrum altogether. All of those are valuable to Janine.

** Stakeholders Hide Their Dreams, Film At Eleven

Unfortunately, many stakeholders do not clearly share the long-term goals they care most intensely about.

# Why not?

# At some time in the past, your stakeholder was given a goal to accomplish. They then spent  time and effort converting that goal into a series of tasks, projects and milestones---aka, "a plan". At some deep level, converting goals to plans is how they understand their job.

# Many stakeholders, having made such a plan, will then proceed to *only* ever talk about the plan. Which means they won't provide you with enough context to propose a /new/ plan: one that still accomplishes their long-term goals, but involves them behaving differently, day-to-day.

# This can leave you very stuck, indeed.

# You need to understand the reasons *why* they're asking you to do various tasks and projects.

# I'll offer a few reasons why stakeholders might fail to clearly communicate goals.

There are a variety of stories stakeholders tell themselves when they fail to clearly communicate goals, including:

# For each, I'll offer an approach for getting past that barrier.

 - *"What You Don't Know Won't Distract You"*

   Some stakeholders deliberately limit what they share in an attempt to encourage you to "focus on what matters". Although this isn't fundamentally a crazy idea, it rarely works for software projects (the intense uncertainty in creating new software means that people on the ground need to be able to change plans often, and to do that, they need lots of underlying context).

 - *"What You Don't Know You Can't Argue With Me About"*

   Unfortunately, some stakeholders feel insecure in their position of authority. Such stakeholders /fear/ getting into a conversation with you about how they mapped from their long-term goal to the tasks they're asking you to do. What if you discover that they're wrong? What if you won't do what they're asking?

 - *"What You Don't Know You Actually Do Know Because I Totally Told You Already"*

   # The final reason that stakeholders won't tell you in detail about their long-term goals is simple: they think they've already done so.

   Your stakeholder thinks about the goal /all the time/. They simply /forget/ that they need to share it clearly and repeatedly with you. In fact, they often believe they *are* sharing it, because when they talk to you about tasks, all they're thinking about is the long-term goals.

# In all three cases, you can mix a pair of tactics to bring your stakeholder's long-term goals out into the open.

** Tactic Two: Make A Usefully Wrong Guess

/Offer your stakeholder a deliberately opinionated guess about their long-term goals, and then cheerfully invite them to tell you why you're wrong./

Why does this one work better than directly asking?

Many people feel safer expressing their thoughts by telling you what's wrong with yours. Putting their own ideas out there presents as vulnerable or risky, whereas criticizing the ideas of others presents as pleasant and empowering. This is maybe not the most admirable characteristic of humans? But it certainly seems a consistent thing, in my experience, at least. So take advantage of it!

For example, you might say:

/"I know every team has to spend effort working on all these security tasks. I've been trying to clarify the outcomes to my team. I could understand the key outcome as just reducing our long-term *risks*. Like, we're *not* expecting to see a short-term bump in sales thanks to acquiring a SOC-2 certification. Is that even vaguely right? Or do I have it totally backwards!?"/

Aim for lightness. You want your tone to clearly indicate: "I'm probably missing something! That's okay! It will be fun for both of us when you correct me!"

With any luck, your stakeholder will respond by saying "/Wait, that's not right. Renewing more enterprise contracts *is* our first goal. You know MonstroCo is threatening to cancel if they don't get a SOC letter this year, right?"/ Of course you don't! This is critical information that you've never heard before! But you don't say that as you cheerfully echo back and summarize what you've just heard.

Now, when you ask your stakeholder to give you a bit more notice before injecting random SOC-2 tasks into your sprints, you can situate that as part of a shared goal of saving the MonstroCo renewal.

A key part of this approach is to that the guess must be *too* opinionated. You want to aim for something which is partly right, but deliberately goes too far. Something which will cause your stakeholder to sit up and take notice. That's vastly more useful than getting them to absently agree to something vague and anodyne.

# Once you've fully drawn your stakeholder out, you'll echo back and summarize your new understanding. The pleasant sounds of their own ideas will make them feel warmly towards you.


# Second, it's easier for people to express their thoughts by telling you what's wrong with yours, than it is to lay their own out.

# Second, many humans like figuring out what they think by telling other people how they're wrong

# Our final reason for offering usefully wrong guesses is that much of the time, humans simply don't know *what* they believe until they hear something that sounds wrong to them.

# When your PM hears you say, /"My understanding is that our top priority is growth, which means acquiring new customers, is that right?"/, something bothers them. They hold up a hand, think for a moment, and then reply:

# /"Wait, that's not it. A good bit of revenue growth has to come from upselling *existing* customers, not just acquiring new ones. That's why we need more compelling features in our Pro tier."/

# You say thank you, and then update your coherent statement and test it again.


# First, spend time coming up with the opinionated guess. Talk to people you work with, review whatever leadership has shared, gather information from wherever you can. Then, distill it all into a single, coherent statement of strategy. Test that out with someone you trust, ask for their feedback, and make it sharper.

# Although you're not aiming to be completely right, you want to be in the right territory. If you're just wildly far off, your stakeholder can feel like they have to do "all the work".

# Second, your statement of strategy *must* be pointed enough that it's possible to disagree with it. The specificity of what you offer is what will prompt your stakeholder to clarify. So push yourself be *very* opinionated.
