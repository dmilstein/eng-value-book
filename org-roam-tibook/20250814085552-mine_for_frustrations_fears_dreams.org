** Intro

In the introduction, we mapped out how, once you've got a proposed adjustment ready to go, you'll actually approach your stakeholder. We call this "Making a Compelling Offer", and it consists of the following steps:

  1. Invoke the strategic outcome

  2. Share your understanding of their concerns

  3. Share your concerns

  4. Propose an adjustment in how you work together

  # 1. Name the strategic, long-term outcome

  # 2. Share an empathetic understanding of their concerns

  # 3. Share your own concerns

  # 4. Propose your adjustment

If you've been following along in the steps in the game plan, you may feel ready to go.

In particular, if you've successfully identified the strategic outcome your stakeholder is working towards, you've hopefully come to better understand two *extremely useful* things:

 - What is the *core challenge* your stakeholder believes your company must face, to achieve that outcome?

 - How exactly do they believe *your team* can help overcome that challenge?

Armed with this knowledge, you may feel like you (finally!) see a clear way to advocate for change.

# A way to express how the current, frustrating pattern of interaction, if left unaddressed, will prevent you *both* from overcoming the core challenge.

In fact, you might look at step 2 of the compelling offer--Share your understanding of their concerns--and say to yourself:

/"Self, we've got this. My stakeholder wants to accomplish that long-term outcome. That's their main 'concern'. Let's go!"/

# If I can talk about how important that outcome is, we should be good to go.

Your self might say back to you:

/"That sounds okay... I guess? But are you *certain* that's all they're concerned about?"/

But you don't hear, so excited are you to tell your stakeholder about why they're not going to be able to achieve their long-term goals unless they stop being so irritating.

Here's the thing. Although the connection from the long-term outcome to the work of your team is, in fact, an excellent foundation for productively challenging your stakeholder...

... it's *not enough*.

Why not? For one simple reason: your stakeholder is a human being. Thus, their behavior is not driven solely by some rational plan to achieve an outcome. We are none of us robots.

In short, your stakeholder has feelings. And those feelings *matter*. To effectively advocate for change, you'll need to develop an *empathetic understanding* of those messy human feelings.

# If you want to persuade your stakeholder to make a change, you're going to need to understand some messy human emotions.

# Thus, the second step in the game plan is to develop an empathetic understanding of your stakeholder's key underlying emotions.

# If you want one of those humans to change their behavior, it's absolutely critical for you to understand their *feelings*.

This is true even if you work with a CEO whose only outward expression of emotion seems to be a tightly clenched jaw.

# This is true even if you and your coworkers engage in serious debates about whether or not your rigidly shut-down Head of Engineering even *has* feelings.

Understanding feelings doesn't mean gathering in a drum circle and getting in touch with our inner child. All humans, in whatever positions of power they may find themselves, have things they deeply want, and things they are deeply afraid of. Your stakeholder's current behavior patterns are driven by some combination of what they want and what they're afraid of.

When you advocate for change, you want to be able to speak with authentic connection to both the fears that are keeping them up at night, as well as the things that they desperately wish were happening.

To bring this to life, let's first see what happens if someone tries to advocate for change, *without* that nuanced, empathetic understanding. Then we'll see how, armed with an understanding of their stakeholder's inner life, they could make a far, far more effective request for change.

** Afford to Ignore

One useful frame is: someone is one of your stakeholders if you *can't afford to ignore them*. That may sound a bit brutally transactional, but if there's too large a set of people who you can't ignore, then there's almost no chance your team can move quickly and create meaningful value.  Asking who you can ignore will sometimes shine a light on the implicit power structures of your company--and can reveal fundamental conflicts that are making it impossible for you to win. E.g. maybe your CEO talks night and day about how critical it is for your team to stay on track for the big new product launch they've promised to the board. However, your team is *also* expected to handle a stream of feature requests from existing customers, which that CEO doesn't pay any attention to. If, one day, the Head of Customer Success comes banging on your door, demanding you immediately add a new feature for enterprise customers, can you *afford* to ignore them? Or if you do, will you receive an unpleasant lecture from the CEO and then acquire a reputation as "not being a team player"?
